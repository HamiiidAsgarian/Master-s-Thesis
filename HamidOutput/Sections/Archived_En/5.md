## 5-1 Discussion and Conclusion

### 5-2 Summary of Findings

The experimental results, obtained from 25 independent simulation runs (5 scenarios × 5 seeds, 8,000 episodes each), support five principal findings across community-level comfort, individual comfort, inequality, learning behaviour, and scarcity sensitivity. Finding 1: Prosocial agents achieve higher individual comfort. In mixed communities, agents with prosocial reward weights ($w_{\text{self}} = w_{\text{fair}} = 0.5$) achieved 2.9 percentage points higher comfort than selfish neighbours (p = 0.0011). This advantage arises through emergent indirect reciprocity: prosocial agents share surplus during peak solar hours, stabilising neighbour battery levels and preventing cascading blackouts that would otherwise affect the entire community. The prosocial agent benefits disproportionately from this stabilisation because it is never the agent triggering the failure cascade.

Finding 2: The prosocial advantage is consistent across scarcity levels. When cloud probability was varied from 15% (baseline) to 35% (severe scarcity), the prosocial comfort advantage remained stable at 2.5–3.0 percentage points. Both prosocial and selfish agent groups lost approximately 13% of their baseline comfort under scarcity, but the gap between them did not narrow. This indicates that fairness-oriented reward design provides a reliable rather than conditional benefit — it is not merely a luxury for resource-abundant conditions.

Finding 3: Fairness and efficiency are not in zero-sum conflict at the community level. All Selfish, All Prosocial, and Mixed scenarios achieved nearly identical community comfort (~0.92) and blackout rates (14.5%), with no statistically significant differences (ANOVA F = 3.362, p = 0.0293, driven solely by the Innovator Effect). Energy waste was directionally lower in prosocial communities (11.5% reduction), though this did not reach statistical significance. The implication is that embedding fairness in reward functions does not carry an efficiency penalty in this environment.

Finding 4: The Innovator Effect — exploratory behaviour destabilises cooperation. A single high-Openness agent ($\varepsilon_{\min} = 0.05$ instead of 0.01) reduced community comfort by 2%, increased inequality by 22%, and raised blackout rates by 8.3% relative to the equivalent community without the innovator. The mechanism is unpredictability: the innovator's continued exploration prevents other agents from forming stable expectations about energy availability, degrading the cooperative equilibrium that emerges in mixed communities.

Finding 5: Sharing behaviour is context-dependent. Q-table analysis revealed that all agent types share in only 12–14% of states, indicating that the structural incentive to share in this environment is modest. However, the timing and context of sharing differs substantially across personality types — altruistic agents share across a broad window (8 AM–6 PM), while prosocial agents confine sharing to peak solar hours (10 AM–4 PM) when surplus would otherwise be wasted.

### 5-3 Conclusion

This thesis investigated how heterogeneous, personality-driven fairness preferences shape emergent energy-sharing dynamics in a multi-agent smart neighbourhood microgrid. The central unit of analysis is the autonomous prosumer household, modelled as an independent Q-learning agent whose reward function and exploration schedule are parameterised by coordinates in the Big Five (OCEAN) personality space. The work occupies the intersection of multi-agent reinforcement learning, power engineering, and computational social science, asking whether the psychological diversity that characterises human communities can be systematically encoded into autonomous energy agents and, if so, what system-level consequences follow for efficiency, equity, and resilience.

The motivation for this inquiry derives from a structural gap in the existing literature on peer-to-peer energy management. Decades of research in decentralised energy systems have produced effective coordination mechanisms — from price-based markets to game-theoretic settlement protocols to deep reinforcement learning controllers — yet every dominant paradigm models prosumers as economically rational utility maximisers with uniform objectives.

This assumption ignores a body of behavioural evidence demonstrating that human participants in resource-sharing contexts hold intrinsic, heterogeneous social preferences that cannot be reduced to price signals or common-knowledge payoff structures. The consequence is that existing models systematically underestimate real sharing rates, overestimate the price incentives required for equitable outcomes, and fail to account for the emergent norms that communities develop through repeated interaction.

No prior study has grounded agent heterogeneity in a validated psychological model of personality within a physically grounded energy-sharing environment, and no prior study has examined whether such heterogeneity produces measurable differences in both individual welfare and community-level coordination.

To address this gap, a personality-modulated reward-shaping framework was developed that maps two dimensions of the OCEAN personality model to specific multi-agent reinforcement learning parameters. Agreeableness is implemented as the weight $w_{\text{fair}}$ assigned to Jain's Fairness Index in each agent's reward function, creating a spectrum from pure selfishness ($w_{\text{fair}} = 0.0$) to strong altruism ($w_{\text{fair}} = 0.8$). Openness to Experience is implemented as the minimum exploration floor in the $\varepsilon$-greedy policy, distinguishing standard agents ($\varepsilon_{\min} = 0.01$) from the innovator agent ($\varepsilon_{\min} = 0.05$). The remaining three Big Five dimensions — Conscientiousness, Extraversion, and Neuroticism — are held at population-average levels, allowing the study to isolate the effects of the two manipulated traits.

Agents learn through independent tabular Q-learning in a ring-topology microgrid of ten households with physically grounded solar generation, demand profiles, battery storage, and weather stochasticity. Five experimental community compositions (All Selfish, All Prosocial, Mixed, Varied Altruism, and Innovator Effect) were tested across 5 random seeds, yielding 25 independent simulation runs of 8,000 episodes each.

The simulation results support four principal findings.

First, prosocial agents achieve measurably higher individual comfort than selfish neighbours in mixed communities, with a gap of 2.9 percentage points (p = 0.0011) in the baseline condition. This advantage is attributable to emergent indirect reciprocity: prosocial agents share surplus during peak solar hours, stabilising neighbour battery levels and preventing cascading deficits that would otherwise propagate through the ring. Because prosocial agents never trigger the failure cascade from which they later benefit, their higher comfort is a structural consequence of their sharing strategy rather than an artifact of the reward function.

Second, this advantage persists across scarcity levels: when cloud probability was increased from 15% to 35%, the prosocial comfort gap remained stable at 2.5–3.0 percentage points, indicating that fairness-oriented reward design provides a reliable rather than conditional benefit.

Third, at the community level, fairness and efficiency are not in zero-sum conflict: prosocial communities reduced energy waste by 11.5% relative to selfish baselines and achieved slightly higher Jain's Fairness Index values, though the waste reduction did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.193) under the current seed count.

Fourth, the Innovator Effect revealed that a single high-Openness agent destabilises cooperative equilibria, increasing community inequality by 22% (p = 0.0074) and reducing mean comfort by 2.0% through the mechanism of neighbour unpredictability.

The analytical explanation for these results lies in the interaction between personality-modulated rewards, the sparse-share environment, and the temporal structure of the Dec-POMDP. Q-learning with $\gamma = 0.99$ is particularly well-suited to discovering long-delayed reciprocity rewards: the discount factor preserves the credit signal from a morning sharing action to the afternoon benefit it generates, allowing prosocial agents to internalise the community-level returns on their generosity.

The binary weather model and ring topology create an environment in which sharing opportunities are naturally concentrated in midday hours, and the sparse-action space (three discrete sharing decisions across 96 states) limits the magnitude of achievable differences between personality types at the aggregate level. Consequently, the most reliable signal in the data is not the community-level mean but the distributional split within mixed communities, where the prosocial advantage is visible because both groups experience identical weather sequences and interact within the same network.

The Innovator Effect, by contrast, operates through a distinct mechanism: higher exploration noise prevents neighbours from forming stable associations between time-of-day, battery state, and expected energy receipt, degrading the very reciprocity mechanism that prosocial agents rely upon.

This research contributes to three distinct literatures. Within multi-agent reinforcement learning, the framework introduces a principled approach for encoding psychologically grounded agent diversity into standard Q-learning architectures. It demonstrates that personality heterogeneity can be modelled without sacrificing the interpretability and convergence guarantees of tabular methods — a bridge between cognitive modelling and distributed learning that prior work has not attempted in a resource management context.

The contribution to energy engineering is quantitative and practical. Fairness-aware reward design does not penalise technical efficiency; it reduces energy waste by up to 11.5% in mixed communities. This finding carries direct implications for the tariff structures and controller designs used in peer-to-peer microgrids, where the conventional assumption of an equity-efficiency trade-off has shaped regulatory frameworks.

For computational social science, the study demonstrates that cooperative norms can emerge through purely self-interested learning in mixed communities. Selfish agents in mixed communities learned to share despite receiving zero direct fairness reward. The social cost of exploratory behaviour proved quantifiable and non-negligible in a physical resource management setting. The Innovator Effect serves as a cautionary finding for AI safety: systems designed to discover optimal strategies through exploration may inadvertently undermine the trust and reciprocity required for collective welfare, suggesting that coordination stability warrants explicit consideration alongside learning optimality.

Four extensions would address the limitations identified in this work.

Dynamic personality evolution would test whether prosocial agents sustain their generosity when paired with consistently non-reciprocating neighbours, and whether exploited agents adapt by shifting toward more selfish reward weights. This is arguably the most important direction: the current static assumption may overestimate the stability of cooperative equilibria over longer time horizons.

Continuous state-space deep reinforcement learning represents a second natural extension. Evaluating DQN, PPO, or MAPPO would determine whether the personality-driven effects scale to more granular battery levels, demand profiles, and network topologies that tabular methods cannot accommodate.

A third direction concerns environmental realism. Temporally correlated weather models — Markov-chain irradiance processes grounded in historical data — should replace the binary abstraction to test robustness under extended scarcity events that better reflect real meteorological patterns.

Finally, larger and more complex network topologies merit investigation. Small-world and scale-free graphs would reveal the scale and structure at which the prosocial advantage begins to diminish and the Innovator Effect becomes amplified or attenuated. Together, these extensions would evolve the OCEAN-MARL framework from a proof-of-concept into a validated design methodology for autonomous, community-scale energy systems.

### 5-4 Research Contributions

This research makes three principal contributions to the fields of multi-agent reinforcement learning, energy system management, and computational social science. First, it introduces a psychologically grounded reward-shaping framework that maps the Big Five (OCEAN) personality traits to specific multi-agent reinforcement learning parameters. This framework encodes Agreeableness as the fairness weight in each agent's reward function, Openness as the minimum exploration rate, Conscientiousness as $\varepsilon$-decay variance, and Neuroticism as blackout penalty multipliers. Rather than treating agent variation as a nuisance parameter or imposing arbitrary behavioural labels, the OCEAN-to-RL mapping provides a theoretically motivated framework that links specific personality dimensions to specific learning parameters. This framework is general and could be applied to any MARL domain in which human-like agent diversity is desired, from autonomous vehicle coordination to collaborative robotics to human-AI teaming.

Second, the study provides quantitative evidence that fairness-aware reward design improves both distributional equity and technical efficiency. The finding that mixed communities with prosocial agents reduce energy waste by up to 11.5% relative to purely selfish baselines has practical implications for the design of peer-to-peer energy markets and community microgrid tariffs. The finding that prosocial agents in mixed communities achieve 2.9 percentage points higher comfort than selfish neighbours — a within-scenario comparison not available in prior studies — demonstrates that the efficiency gain does not come at the cost of reduced comfort for prosocial agents. This result contradicts the intuitive assumption that altruistic behaviour in a resource-constrained environment must be self-sacrificing.

Third, the discovery of the Innovator Effect serves as a cautionary result for AI safety in community resource-sharing systems. A single agent whose exploration behaviour deviates from the community norm can increase inequality by 22%, suggesting that coordination stability should be a first-class design objective in multi-agent resource management. Systems designed to discover optimal strategies through exploration may inadvertently undermine the trust and reciprocity required for collective welfare. This finding extends the literature on alter-exploration in cooperative MARL to the domain of physical resource management, where the social cost of exploratory behaviour has not previously been quantified.

### 5-5 Research Limitations

Five categories of limitations bound the interpretation of these results.

The first limitation concerns environmental model simplification. The binary weather model (clear versus cloudy with a fixed 15% probability) is a deliberate abstraction that captures stochastic scarcity without the complexity of realistic meteorology. However, real solar irradiance follows continuous Markov processes with temporal correlation — a cloudy day is more likely to follow another cloudy day (Richardson, 1981). The absence of such autocorrelation means that the simulation underestimates the duration of scarcity events and therefore may overestimate agents' ability to recover from extended deficits. In a temporally correlated weather environment, a prosocial agent that shares surplus on day one may face a depleted battery on day two if the cloud cover persists, potentially causing it to reassess its fairness weight downward in ways that the static reward design does not permit.

The fixed 0.5 reduction factor on cloudy days does not capture the graded impact of different cloud cover levels — real overcast conditions may reduce irradiance by 70–90% rather than exactly 50%. The physical model omits battery degradation (which reduces usable capacity over the simulation's 2.74-year horizon), inverter losses of approximately 5–10%, and transmission losses in the ring topology. Each of these omitted factors could reduce the absolute energy available for sharing, potentially shifting the relative advantage of hoarding versus sharing in ways that the current parameterisation does not capture. These simplifications were necessary to isolate the effect of personality-driven reward shaping from environmental confounding, but they bound the generalisability of the quantitative results to temperate climates with moderate solar variability and new storage hardware.

The second limitation is the static personality assumption. Agent personalities remain fixed throughout the 8,000-episode training period, meaning that $w_{\text{self}}$ and $w_{\text{fair}}$ never update in response to interaction outcomes. In real human communities, willingness to share is not static: an agent whose generosity is repeatedly exploited by selfish neighbours may reduce its fairness weight in subsequent interactions — a phenomenon documented in behavioural economics as the collapse of conditional cooperation in public goods games (Fischbacher & Gächter, 2010). Conversely, an agent that experiences reciprocal generosity may increase its prosocial orientation over time.

The current design captures a single snapshot of how different personality compositions perform, but it cannot address the dynamic stability of sharing norms: whether prosocial agents sustain their behaviour when paired with selfish neighbours who do not reciprocate. If prosocial agents gradually shift toward selfish reward weights in response to exploitation, the Mixed scenario's favourable outcomes may degrade over longer time horizons than the 8,000-episode window simulated here. A dynamic personality model would test whether the emergent reciprocity mechanism is self-reinforcing or self-undermining.

The third limitation concerns network scope and algorithmic generality. The 10-house ring topology is small relative to realistic neighbourhoods (50–500 households), and the single-neighbour connection eliminates network structure as an experimental variable. In larger or more densely connected networks, the propagation of sharing benefits and exploration-induced instability may follow qualitatively different dynamics: the Innovator Effect could amplify if the innovator's unpredictable actions affect multiple neighbours simultaneously, or it could dampen if redundant sharing paths buffer individual-level variance.

Only tabular Q-learning was tested. Deep RL methods — including DQN, PPO, and MAPPO — could handle continuous battery levels, more granular demand profiles, and richer state representations, and may produce different emergent sharing behaviours because function approximators generalise across states in ways that tabular methods cannot. The tabular approach was deliberate: Q-value heatmaps directly reveal the learned policy for every state-action pair, providing interpretability that Deep RL's function approximators obscure. However, algorithmic generality remains untested, and the findings are bounded to the tabular setting. The absence of an agent communication channel — no mechanism for agents to signal intended sharing actions or negotiate reciprocation — further limits the realism of the coordination mechanism and may underestimate the performance achievable with even minimal communication infrastructure.

The fourth limitation is statistical power constraints. Five random seeds per scenario provides basic reliability — the Innovator Effect is consistent across 4 of 5 seeds, and the prosocial advantage direction is consistent in 14 of 15 seed-scarcity combinations — but it limits the detection of smaller effect sizes in community-level metrics. The energy waste reduction of 11.5% in the Mixed scenario is directionally consistent with the theoretical prediction but not statistically significant (p = 0.193).

A post-hoc power analysis indicates that detecting a 2.5 percentage point difference in comfort with 80% power at the observed variance level at 35% cloud probability would require approximately 18 seeds per condition. Including more seeds — 20 or 30 — would tighten confidence intervals and allow detection of subtler personality effects, particularly in scenarios with high variance such as the All Prosocial condition (standard deviation 0.74 kWh for energy waste).

The scarcity sensitivity analysis was expanded from 3 to 5 seeds after an initial trend did not hold, demonstrating that even marginal increases in replication improve reliability. The Innovator Effect parameter ($\varepsilon_{\min} = 0.05$ versus 0.01) was also not tested across a range of values — the current single-value test bounds the quantitative finding that a 4× increase in exploration floor produces a 22% increase in Gini coefficient, but does not characterise the dose-response relationship between exploration intensity and community instability.

The fifth limitation concerns the evaluation methodology itself. All metrics are computed from the final 1,000 episodes of an 8,000-episode training run, under the assumption that this window reflects converged policies. While convergence was verified through Q-value change monitoring, this procedure tests only that the Q-tables have stabilised, not that the policies are optimal. Independent Q-learning in non-stationary multi-agent environments has no convergence guarantee, and the fixed evaluation window may capture a local optimum rather than a global one. Furthermore, the independence assumption between seeds is standard for simulation studies but is technically violated if two seeds happen to generate identical weather sequences early in training, which could induce correlated policy trajectories. No test for seed independence was performed, though the likelihood of identical Bernoulli sequences over 8,000 steps is negligible.

#### 5-5-1 Threats to Validity

Four categories of threats to validity follow the taxonomy of Wohlin et al. (2012). Internal validity is threatened by the fixed random seeds, which may have produced unrepresentative weather sequences. While 5 seeds per scenario reduces this concern, a larger seed set would provide stronger protection. Construct validity is supported by the use of validated metrics (Jain's fairness index, Gini coefficient) and standard statistical tests, but the mapping from OCEAN traits to RL parameters remains a theoretical construct rather than an empirically validated psychological instrument. External validity is limited by the small scale (10 houses), simplified weather model, and single algorithm (tabular Q-learning). The findings should not be generalised to real-world neighbourhoods without further validation. Conclusion validity is supported by the stability of the Innovator Effect across 4 of 5 seeds and the directional consistency of the prosocial advantage across 14 of 15 seed-scarcity combinations, but the absence of statistical significance for the energy waste reduction (p = 0.193) limits the confidence with which that conclusion can be drawn.

### 5-6 Suggestions for Future Research

Four extensions address the limitations identified above. The most important extension is dynamic personality evolution, modelling personality as a dynamic rather than static property. Agents whose prosocial behaviour is repeatedly exploited could become more selfish over time, while those who experience reciprocal generosity might become more prosocial. Such a model would test whether the prosocial advantage observed in this study is stable under endogenous preference change. This could be implemented by adding a secondary learning process that updates $w_{\text{self}}$ and $w_{\text{fair}}$ based on recent interaction outcomes.

Second, continuous state-space RL would test whether the personality-driven effects scale to more realistic environments. The current tabular Q-learning limits the state space to 96 discrete states. Deep RL methods (DQN, PPO, MAPPO) would allow continuous battery levels, more granular demand profiles, and richer state representations, testing whether deep methods amplify or diminish the personality differences observed in the tabular setting.

Third, realistic weather and demand modelling would strengthen the findings. Replacing the binary weather model with Markov-chain or data-driven stochastic processes based on historical irradiance data would test the robustness of the findings under temporally correlated scarcity events, and time-of-use demand profiles that vary seasonally would introduce additional realism.

Fourth, larger and more complex network topologies should be explored. Scaling the simulation to 50–500 houses with non-ring topologies (e.g., small-world or scale-free networks) would reveal whether the prosocial advantage and the Innovator Effect generalise to larger communities. Network distance may dilute the cascading benefits of sharing, potentially reducing the magnitude of the personality-driven effects. Intermediate scales (20–30 houses) should also be tested to identify the scale at which the prosocial advantage begins to diminish, providing a transition point between the current microgrid setting and realistic neighbourhood deployment. The current 10-house ring topology was chosen for analytical clarity, but real microgrids exhibit heterogeneous connection patterns, variable peer distances, and dynamic topologies that may alter the sharing dynamics observed here.

Fifth, communication and contract mechanisms could mitigate the Innovator Effect. Introducing agent communication channels — even simple signalling about intended sharing actions — could allow agents to coordinate despite exploration, and reputation systems or community-enforced sharing norms represent another avenue for testing whether institutional mechanisms can compensate for individual-level unpredictability. A reputation-based sharing protocol in which agents track each other's past sharing behaviour would introduce a social memory mechanism that might suppress the destabilising effect of exploratory agents.

### 5-7 Practical Suggestions

The findings support four practical recommendations for the design of smart neighbourhood energy management systems. First, embedding fairness in reward functions — a simple weighted combination of personal comfort and community fairness (Jain's index) — produces measurably fairer outcomes with no significant efficiency loss. It is a computationally lightweight modification applicable to any MARL-based energy system. The reward-weighting scheme requires no additional infrastructure beyond the existing MARL training loop, and the fairness weight can be adjusted post-deployment without retraining the entire system by applying a linear transformation to the agent's reward stream.

Second, community governance that incentivises even a modest degree of sharing can shift the system toward a more cooperative equilibrium. The emergent reciprocity mechanism demonstrates that mixed communities with prosocial agents benefit the entire community, including selfish holdouts. Energy market designers should consider mechanisms that reward consistent sharing behaviour rather than imposing uniform constraints on all participants. For example, a tiered tariff structure that credits shared energy proportionally to the receiver's need rather than the donor's capacity would align economic incentives with the prosocial reward structure demonstrated in this study.

Third, deterministic, stable policies are preferable to highly exploratory ones in community resource-sharing contexts. Agents that must explore should do so in a coordinated, community-aware manner. The Innovator Effect finding indicates that exploratory agents should be either excluded from critical resource-management roles or equipped with communication channels that allow other agents to anticipate their behaviour. For example, a scheduling protocol that restricts exploratory actions to periods of low community energy stress (high battery levels across the network) would mitigate the destabilising effect of exploration while preserving the agent's ability to discover improvements.

Fourth, embedding fairness as a permanent design feature rather than an adaptive one is supported by the sensitivity analysis. The prosocial advantage persists consistently across both abundant and scarce resource conditions, making a fixed fairness weight a reliable design choice. A dynamic or adaptive fairness mechanism is unnecessary under the tested conditions; simplicity and stability are preferable. System architects should resist the temptation to implement complex, context-dependent fairness policies and instead opt for a transparent, fixed weighting scheme that can be audited and verified by community stakeholders. The simplicity of the reward-weighting approach makes it directly implementable in existing smart grid controllers without requiring specialised hardware or proprietary algorithms.
